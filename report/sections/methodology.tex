\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
 
\subsection{Design Philosophy and Guiding Principles}
\label{sec:design-philosophy}
 
The Related Work identified several gaps in the current landscape of
CTF-based cybersecurity education: a narrow focus on classical
vulnerability classes with modern exploit categories rarely
represented~\cite{Sasiwanit}, limited empirical evidence for
measurable learning gain within graded web-specific
coursework~\cite{Meinsma2022}, unresolved challenges around secure
distribution of containerised challenges with embedded
secrets~\cite{raj2016}, and a nascent understanding of how LLM-based
solvers interact with multi-stage exploitation
tasks~\cite{ji2025}. This section describes the methodology by which
these gaps were translated into a concrete suite of web
exploitation CTF challenges, beginning with the design philosophy that
governed every subsequent architectural and pedagogical decision. The
remainder of the Methodology then details the challenge
implementations (Section~\ref{sec:challenge-design}), the
academic integrity infrastructure
(Section~\ref{sec:flag-personalisation}), the containerisation
strategy (Section~\ref{sec:containerisation}), and the
verification, validation, and testing methodology
(Section~\ref{sec:vvt}) whose results are presented in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.
 
Five core principles were established at the outset of the project and
applied consistently across all challenges. Each principle
addresses a specific finding from the literature and imposes a
measurable constraint on the design. Table~\ref{tab:design-principles}
summarises the mapping between principles and their motivating sources;
the paragraphs that follow elaborate on each in turn.
 
\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\label{tab:design-principles}

\textbf{Principle} & \textbf{Motivating Literature} \\ \hline

\subsection{Challenge Architecture Overview}
\label{sec:challenge-architecture}

Before examining each challenge in detail, this section presents the
full suite as a single system, mapping the challenges against
the OWASP Top~10 taxonomy, the three-tier deliverable structure
defined in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, and the shared
infrastructure that underpins per-user personalisation and portable
deployment.

Several design properties are visible from the table. First, no two
challenges share the same primary technology stack: the suite spans
Node.js, PHP/Laravel, Python/Flask, and Rust/Actix-web on the backend,
with EJS server-side rendering, React single-page applications, and
Jinja2 templating on the frontend. As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:design-philosophy}, this diversity is a deliberate
pedagogical constraint. Second, the number of flags per challenge
increases with tier: the Basic challenges each contain a single flag
obtained through a short exploit chain, while the Advanced challenges
each contain four flags arranged in a progressive chain where each
flag teaches a discrete skill and reveals information required for the
next. Third, the OWASP coverage is distributed across the suite rather
than concentrated in a single challenge, ensuring that a student who
completes the full suite has engaged with a broad cross-section of the
taxonomy.

Four categories (A04, A06, A08, A09) are not covered by the suite.
This is a conscious scope decision rather than an oversight. A04

six covered categories collectively account for the majority of
real-world web application vulnerabilities reported in OWASP's
incidence data~\cite{OWASP2025}, and the three distinct injection
sub-types provide depth within the highest-impact category.
 
\subsubsection{Tiered Structure and Deliverable Mapping}
 
The three difficulty tiers correspond directly to the deliverable
structure defined in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, but with an
important clarification of terminology. The terms \textit{Basic},
\textit{Intermediate}, and \textit{Advanced} describe the
\textit{challenge difficulty}, not the project deliverable complexity.
At the deliverable level, the project's Advanced tier encompasses the
testing, packaging, and distribution infrastructure that supports the
entire suite, rather than referring only to the hardest challenges.
Table~\ref{tab:tier-mapping} clarifies this relationship.

Project-wide deliverables (applying to all/most CTFs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Full Dockerisation as distributable stacks
    \item Automated end-to-end testing
    \item LLM-based difficulty validation and Human participant evaluation
\end{itemize}

The Basic tier is designed to be accessible to students with no prior
CTF experience, requiring only familiarity with browser developer
tools and basic HTTP concepts (and adequate research). The Intermediate tier assumes the
skills developed in the Basic tier and adds the requirement to chain
multiple techniques within a single challenge, synthesising
information disclosed at one stage to enable exploitation at the next.
The Advanced tier demands the additional ability to develop bypass
techniques against active defences (WAF keyword filters, protocol
restrictions) and to reason about internal service architectures that
are not directly visible from the application's user-facing surface.
This progression is designed to maintain Cs\'{i}kszentmih\'{a}lyi's
flow state~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi} across the full skill range of the
target cohort, and its effectiveness is assessed through the human
participant evaluation described in Section~\ref{sec:vvt}.

generation, credential management, and containerised deployment.

This three-layer separation ensures that the concerns of user

challenge's vulnerability design can be updated without affecting the
generation or containerisation layers, provided the flag injection
interface is preserved.



\subsection{Per-User Flag Personalisation and Academic Integrity Mechanisms}
\label{sec:flag-personalisation}

\subsubsection{Threat Model}

The existing Networks and Systems module distributes nearly identical
virtual machines containing a fixed set of challenges with randomised
flags. This architecture is vulnerable to three
distinct integrity threats. First, \textbf{solution sharing}: because
every student faces an identical vulnerability surface, a single
working exploit can be communicated to the entire cohort. The shared
exploit requires no adaptation, so the recipient gains the flag
without engaging with the underlying security concepts. Second,
\textbf{flag copying}: even where flags are randomised per instance,
if the randomisation is performed at VM build time and the same VM
image is distributed, flags are static across sessions. A student who
obtains another student's flag through any channel (messaging,
observation, or screenshot) can submit it directly. Third,
\textbf{LLM-assisted solving}: uniform problem structures that
closely resemble publicly documented vulnerabilities can be solved by
prompting a language model with the challenge description. Ji et
al.~\cite{ji2025} demonstrate that with appropriate scaffolding, LLMs
can achieve over 80\% performance improvement on standard CTF
datasets, making this threat increasingly practical.

The flag personalisation system described in this section addresses
the second and third threats directly, and provides partial mitigation
for the first. The first threat (methodology sharing) is addressed
structurally through the multi-stage exploit chain design discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:design-philosophy}: chains requiring four sequential
exploitation steps produce solution paths that are difficult to
communicate concisely, unlike single-step challenges.

\subsubsection{Flag Generation Algorithm}

The core flag generation algorithm used by six of the seven challenges
(CTF1, CTF2, CTF3, CTF5, CTF6, CTF7) is deterministic derivation via
HMAC-SHA256~\cite{rfc2104}. Given a per-challenge salt $s$ and a
student username $u$, the flag token is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\textit{token} = \text{HMAC-SHA256}(s, \; u)\text{.hex}[{:}N]
\label{eq:hmac-flag}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $N$ is the token length in hexadecimal characters and
the \texttt{.hex} notation denotes the full digest rendered as a
lowercase hexadecimal string. The final flag is assembled as:

\begin{equation}
\textit{flag} = \textit{prefix}\texttt{\{}\textit{token}\texttt{\_}\textit{username}\texttt{\}}
\label{eq:flag-format}
\end{equation}

\noindent For example, with prefix \texttt{durham}, salt
\texttt{basic1-default-salt}, username \texttt{abcd12}, and $N = 16$,
the generator produces a flag of the form
\texttt{durham\{3a1f9c2b4e7d8a0f\_abcd12\}}. The username is embedded
in the flag itself, enabling attribution: any submitted flag
immediately identifies the student for whom it was generated.

HMAC-SHA256 was selected for three properties. First,
\textbf{determinism}: the same username and salt always produce the
same token, meaning flags can be regenerated at any time without
requiring persistent state. If a \texttt{flags.json} file is lost or
corrupted, it can be reconstructed from the student list and the
deployment salt alone. Second, \textbf{collision resistance}: even
truncated to 16 hexadecimal characters (64 bits of entropy), the
probability of two distinct usernames producing the same token under a
given salt is approximately $2^{-32}$ by the birthday
bound~\cite{HMACNIST}, which is negligible for cohort sizes of
100--200 students. Third, \textbf{deployment isolation}: changing the
salt produces an entirely different flag space, so flags generated for
one deployment (e.g.\ the 2025--26 academic year) are invalid in
another (e.g.\ 2026--27), even for the same student.

The generator architecture is pluggable: each challenge provides a
module in \texttt{generators/<n>\_generator.js} that exports a
function \texttt{(username, options) $\rightarrow$ token}. The
\texttt{options} object carries the salt and token length, both of
which can be overridden via environment variables
(\texttt{GENERATOR\_SALT}, \texttt{GENERATOR\_TOKEN\_LENGTH}). This
design allows a module administrator to change the flag space for an
entire cohort by setting a single environment variable, without
modifying any source code.

\subsubsection{Token Length and Entropy Evolution}

The token length was increased from 16 hexadecimal characters (64-bit
entropy) in CTF1 to 20 hexadecimal characters (80-bit entropy) in
CTF2 onwards. Table~\ref{tab:flag-params} summarises the per-challenge
parameters.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\label{tab:flag-params}
\begin{tabular}{|c|l|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{CTF} & \textbf{Prefix} & \textbf{Token} & \textbf{Entropy} \\ \hline
1 & \texttt{durham}      & 16 hex & 64-bit \\ \hline
2 & \texttt{durham-pm}   & 20 hex & 80-bit \\ \hline
3 & \texttt{durham-hr}   & 20 hex & 80-bit \\ \hline
5 & \texttt{durham-cms}  & 20 hex & 80-bit \\ \hline

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The increase from 64 to 80 bits raises the cost of brute-force
token guessing from approximately $2^{63}$ expected attempts to
$2^{79}$, a factor of roughly $10^{4.8}$. While 64-bit tokens are
already computationally infeasible to brute-force for a student
adversary, the increase was adopted as a precaution for the
Intermediate and Advanced tiers, where students possess greater
technical sophistication and where the multi-flag structure provides
more opportunities to observe token patterns. CTF7 reverts to 16-character
tokens because it is a single-flag basic challenge where the additional
entropy provides no meaningful security benefit.

\subsubsection{Multi-Flag Salt Derivation}

Challenges containing multiple flags (CTF3, CTF5, CTF6) require an
additional design consideration: if all flags for a given user were
derived from the same salt, capturing one flag would reveal
information about the derivation that could theoretically aid in
guessing another. This is addressed through \textbf{per-flag salt
parameterisation}. For CTF5 and CTF6, each of the four flags uses a
distinct salt derived from a base salt and the flag number:

\begin{equation}
s_i = \textit{base\_salt}\texttt{-flag}i, \quad i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}
\label{eq:per-flag-salt}
\end{equation}

\noindent For example, CTF5 uses salts \texttt{novacms-ctf5-2026-flag1}
through \texttt{novacms-ctf5-2026-flag4}. Because HMAC-SHA256 with
different keys produces statistically independent outputs, knowing
one flag provides zero information about any other flag for the same
user. CTF3 uses a similar approach with two distinct salts for its
two HMAC-derived flags (\texttt{ctf3-api-flag-salt-2026} and
\texttt{ctf3-decrypt-flag-salt-2026}).

\subsubsection{Challenge-Specific Variations}

Two challenges introduce additional layers on top of the core HMAC
algorithm to serve specific pedagogical purposes.

\paragraph{CTF3: AES-256-CBC encryption layer.} One of CTF3's flags
is encrypted after generation. The HMAC-derived plaintext flag is
encrypted using AES-256-CBC, where the encryption key is derived by
applying SHA-256 to a passphrase that students must discover through
source code inspection. A random initialisation vector
(\texttt{crypto.randomBytes(16)}) is generated for each encryption
operation, meaning the ciphertext is non-deterministic even though
the underlying flag is deterministic. The output format is
\texttt{iv\_base64:ciphertext\_base64}. This design is deliberate:
the encryption itself is part of the challenge, requiring students to
identify the key material, understand the cipher mode, and implement
decryption. The non-deterministic ciphertext also means that even if
two students share the encrypted blob, they cannot confirm whether
their underlying flags are the same without performing the
decryption.

\paragraph{CTF4: Non-HMAC flag generation.} CTF4 is the sole
challenge that does not use the HMAC-based generator. Its flags
follow the format \texttt{CTF\{dom\_xss\_\$\{username\}\_\$\{Date.now()\}\}},
incorporating a timestamp that makes each flag instance unique not only per
user but per generation event. CTF4 also uses a different deployment
model: rather than injecting flags into a shared application, it generates
per-user Docker Compose override files with isolated port mappings, creating
fully separated challenge instances. This represents a fundamentally different
approach to personalisation, one that varies the deployment environment
itself rather than only the flag values. The trade-off is significantly
higher infrastructure complexity: each student requires a separate Docker
stack, which limits scalability relative to the shared-instance model used
by the other challenges.

\paragraph{CTF6: Bootstrap script generation.} In addition to
standard flag generation, CTF6's generator produces a synthetic cloud
user-data bootstrap script that embeds Flag~2 within a realistic-looking
instance initialisation payload. This script is served by the
simulated AWS metadata endpoint and is the artefact that students
discover through SSRF exploitation. The generated script is unique per
user because the embedded flag is derived from the user's HMAC token,
maintaining the personalisation guarantee through the SSRF exploit chain.

\subsubsection{Credential Generation Pipeline}

Alongside flag generation, the system produces per-user credentials
through the \texttt{generate\_credentials.js} module. Usernames
follow the format \texttt{[a-z]\{4\}[0-9]\{2\}} (e.g.\
\texttt{abcd12}), enforced by regex validation across all generators.
This format was chosen to be short enough for students to type
reliably while remaining sufficiently structured to avoid collisions
in small cohorts. Passwords are generated non-deterministically using
\texttt{crypto.randomBytes()} and rendered as hexadecimal (or base64
for CTF5), producing 8--12 character strings with high entropy.
Because passwords are non-deterministic, re-running the generator
produces new passwords and requires restarting the associated
challenge services.

Default user roles vary by challenge to match each scenario's
narrative context: \texttt{user} for CTF1, CTF2, and CTF7;
\texttt{editor} for CTF5 (a content management system); and
\texttt{analyst} for CTF6 (a security consultancy). CTF3 generates
the richest credential bundles, including an employee identifier,
department, position, hire date, and monthly salary alongside the
authentication credentials, as these fields populate the HR system's
employee database and form part of the SQL injection target surface.

The generated credential bundles (\texttt{credentials.json}) flow
into each challenge's data layer through stack-appropriate mechanisms:
direct JSON file loading at startup for Node.js and Flask challenges
(CTF1, CTF2, CTF5), database seeding via Laravel's
\texttt{php artisan migrate:fresh -{}-seed} for CTF3, Docker
Compose environment variable injection for CTF4, and combined
JSON and per-user text file output for CTF7 (where the exploit reads
flags from individual files on disk via \texttt{fs.readFileSync()}).
Table~\ref{tab:flag-flow} summarises the data flow for each challenge.

\subsubsection{Integrity Properties}

The flag personalisation system addresses each threat identified in
the threat model as follows.

\paragraph{Flag sharing becomes attributable.} Because the username is
embedded in the flag string itself
(Equation~\ref{eq:flag-format}), every submitted flag identifies its
legitimate owner. A student who submits a flag containing another
student's username is immediately identifiable. This transforms flag
sharing from an undetectable activity into a detectable and
attributable one, shifting the deterrence calculus: the risk of
detection now accompanies any attempt to share flags.

\paragraph{Flag reuse across deployments is impossible.} The
salt-based derivation ensures that flags generated under one
deployment salt are invalid under another. An instructor deploying the
same challenges in a subsequent academic year need only change the
salt to invalidate all previously generated flags, including any
that may have been shared through unofficial channels or published
in online write-ups.

\paragraph{LLM-generated flags are invalid.} Because flags are derived
from a secret salt that is never exposed to the challenge
application's user-facing surface, there is no information available
to an LLM (or any other external tool) from which a valid flag could
be constructed. An LLM may be able to guide a student through the
exploitation methodology, but it cannot produce the flag itself
without the student actually executing the exploit against their
personalised instance. This property is evaluated empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:results} through systematic LLM testing.

\subsubsection{Scope and Limitations}

The personalisation implemented in this project is deliberately
scoped to the flag layer. Full SecGen-style environment
randomisation~\cite{chothia2023}, which varies network ports, service
configurations, and vulnerability profiles per student, was considered
but not adopted for two reasons. First, the scope constraint: each
challenge uses a distinct technology stack, and implementing
per-student environment variation across seven different stacks would
have required infrastructure investment disproportionate to the
integrity benefit. Second, as argued in
Section~\ref{sec:design-philosophy}, for web exploitation challenges
the primary integrity risk is flag sharing rather than methodology
sharing. A student who can articulate how to exploit a SQL injection
filter bypass has demonstrated the intended learning outcome regardless
of whether they arrived at the methodology independently or through
discussion with peers. The per-user flag mechanism therefore targets
the highest-impact threat without imposing the full architectural
overhead of environment randomisation, occupying a pragmatic middle
ground between simple flag randomisation and comprehensive
per-student environment generation.
