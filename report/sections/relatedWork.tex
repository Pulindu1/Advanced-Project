\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\subsection{Scope of the Review}
This section surveys published work across five areas that directly
inform the design and evaluation of the challenge suite: CTF
pedagogy and its integration into graded coursework; progressive
difficulty and multi-stage exploit design; web application
vulnerability taxonomies, with particular attention to OWASP
coverage; containerised challenge distribution; and the emerging
use of large language models (LLMs) as automated CTF solvers. For
each area the review extracts the key findings, evaluates the
methodological limitations of the cited work, and states the gap
that this project addresses. The goal is not to exhaustively list
every prior CTF platform but to foreground the specific prior
findings that each design decision in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} either builds on or deliberately
departs from.


\subsection{CTF Pedagogy and Experiential Learning}
Capture-the-Flag competitions have become widely adopted as a
pedagogical tool in cybersecurity education. Leune and Petrilli
\cite{KeesPetrelli} report that CTF participation enhances technical
proficiency, student engagement, and self-confidence, and argue that
such exercises bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and
real-world practice in line with Dewey's principle that practical
and theoretical instruction must proceed together~\cite{Dewey1938}.
The study draws primarily on self-reported student attitudes rather
than on a controlled experimental design, however, which weakens the
strength of its causal claims and invites triangulation against
process-based evidence.

Meinsma et al.~\cite{Meinsma2022} provide a measurement framework
linking Jeopardy-style challenges to explicit knowledge, skill, and
attitude objectives using the CTFd platform~\cite{CTFd}. Their
results pointedly challenge the assumption that participation
automatically builds competence: they find no consistent evidence of
measurable learning growth and no reliable correlation between
performance and self-reported enthusiasm. They conclude that logging
data alone cannot reveal how or why learning occurs. The implication
for the present project is that flag capture counts are a necessary
but insufficient outcome measure, and that challenge design must
additionally expose process evidence (e.g.\ captured console logs,
intermediate artefacts produced during exploitation) from which
skill development can be inferred.

The tension between these positions highlights a gap in empirical
evidence for learning gain within graded, web-specific coursework.
Vykopal et al.~\cite{Vykopal} address the integration question by
identifying four risks when embedding CTFs into formal assessments:
improper scoring, insufficient scaffolding, plagiarism, and limited
analytics. The present project addresses the plagiarism pitfall
directly through HMAC-personalised flags
(Section~\ref{sec:flag-personalisation}) and the scaffolding
pitfall through the per-CTF \texttt{STORY.md} breadcrumb
chain (Section~\ref{sec:design-philosophy}); the scoring and
analytics pitfalls remain delegated to the host module's
existing infrastructure (CTFd-compatible byte-match flags,
per-stack server logs), and a learning-aware markscheme that
would close the analytics pitfall is identified as further
work in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

Effective challenge design frequently combines
Csikszentmih{\'a}lyi's Flow
Theory~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi}, which holds that optimal
engagement arises when challenge difficulty matches participant
skill, with practitioner-driven maxims such as realism, fairness,
and ``learning as the path to the flag''~\cite{Fuzyll2015,Balaji2025}.
The present project adopts a tiered progression (from basic cookie
tampering in CTF1 to an SSRF chain pivoting through simulated cloud
infrastructure in CTF6) to maintain this flow across a heterogeneous
cohort, and uses the OWASP Top~10~\cite{OWASP2025} as the external
coverage framework. A dual evaluation combining automated exploit
scripts and a targeted human walkthrough is used to address the
process-evidence gap identified by Meinsma et al.



\subsection{Progressive Difficulty and Multi-Stage Exploits}
Balaji et al.~\cite{Balaji2025} describe a structured learning path
in which each flag unlocks the next level, progressing from basic
to advanced topics. Their sequential-gating model aligns with the
well-established pedagogical principle of scaffolding: each stage
requires and thereby certifies competence before the next becomes
accessible. Their platform, however, spans cryptography, reverse
engineering, forensics, and web exploitation simultaneously, which
means that the difficulty gradient operates \textit{across} domains
rather than \textit{within} a single domain. That is appropriate for
general CTF training but under-serves a course that wishes to build
web-specific competence in depth.

The present project adopts a hybrid position: the suite as a whole
uses between-challenge gating similar to Balaji et al., but within
the Hard-difficulty challenges (CTF5, CTF6, CTF9) a within-domain
progression is introduced, where multiple sequential flags teach
discrete sub-skills (e.g.\ SSTI information disclosure, then
Jinja2 filter bypass via hex encoding, then
\texttt{\_\_globals\_\_} traversal to remote code execution). This lets a single scenario carry a complete
micro-curriculum on a single vulnerability class, rather than
asking the student to switch domains at every level.

A complementary motivation for graded difficulty within a single
suite is \textit{cohort-scale calibration}: a Networks-and-Systems
module wishing to assign the same challenge corpus to a mixed-
ability cohort needs Easy challenges that pass the median student
unaided (so the assignment is not punitive at the lower bound) and
Hard challenges that retain pedagogical headroom for the strongest
students (so the assignment is not trivial at the upper bound).
The same graded structure makes the suite naturally compatible
with a cohort-scoring or modest-incentive evaluation regime
(cash or gift-card prizes for top scores, an optional
not-for-credit run), which the lightweight literature on CTF
participation incentives identifies as an effective recruitment
device for both pedagogy and evaluation~\cite{Vykopal}; this
project's evaluation does not implement such a regime, but the
graded design admits it without further redesign.

Fuzyll's practitioner maxims~\cite{Fuzyll2015} argue that challenges
should reward understanding over guesswork, and that learning
should be the path to the flag rather than a coincidental by-product.
This motivates a deliberate design decision in the present project:
each challenge embeds multiple discovery paths that lead to the
same exploit (for example, CTF5's Flag~2 key material is signalled
both through an HTML comment in the CMS editor and through an
intentional CHANGELOG entry), so that players approaching the
problem from different angles can still reach the intended learning
outcome. The trade-off is that multiple discovery paths increase
the risk of unintended solutions, which must be mitigated through
post-design vulnerability auditing. This project treats the auditing
step as a first-class engineering activity, recording its findings
in a standardised \textit{Post-Design Audit} appendix appended to
each challenge's \texttt{SOLUTIONS.md}.


\subsection{Web Application Vulnerabilities and OWASP Coverage}
The selection of vulnerability classes for the challenge suite is
grounded in the OWASP Top~10~\cite{OWASP2025}, the most widely
adopted taxonomy of web application security risks. Sadat et al.
\cite{sadat2024} survey common web application vulnerabilities and
countermeasures and confirm that classical flaws (SQLi, XSS, broken
authentication) remain the most prevalent targets for defensive
training. Nawrocki et al.~\cite{nawrocki} provide a complementary
OWASP-mapped analysis. Both papers are useful for justifying the
inclusion of foundational vulnerability classes in CTFs 1--4, but
neither extends the analysis to emerging classes such as SSTI and
SSRF, and neither reports measured learning outcomes.

The suite designed here covers eight of the ten 2021 OWASP
categories (A01, A02, A03, A05, A07, A08, A09, A10), and
represents three fundamentally different injection types within
A03 alone: SQL injection at the database layer, DOM-based XSS
at the browser rendering layer, and server-side template
injection at the application's view layer. This breadth is wider than the four challenge types
(Hidden Information, Brute Force, SQLi, XSS) implemented in
the JMeter-tested system of Sasiwanit et al.~\cite{Sasiwanit};
the under-representation of SSTI, insecure deserialisation,
and SSRF in educational platforms more broadly is consistent
with the pedagogy literature
review~\cite{Svabensky2021,Vykopal}. The 2025 OWASP Top~10 introduces additional
categories including \textit{Software Supply Chain Failures}
and \textit{Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions}, and
elevates Security Misconfiguration in the rankings; these
2025-specific classes are not directly modelled in the present
suite, and a 2025 refresh is identified as further work in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

Two vulnerability classes warrant particular discussion because they
are under-represented in CTF education and are central to this
suite.

Server-Side Template Injection (SSTI) falls under the Injection
category and occurs when user input is embedded unsafely into a
template engine's rendering pipeline. Unlike SQL injection, which
targets the database, SSTI targets the view layer and can escalate
directly to remote code execution by traversing the template
engine's object hierarchy~\cite{PortSwiggerSSTI,JinjaMRO}. Check
Point Research~\cite{CheckPointSSTI2024} reports that as of 2024
an average of one in sixteen organisations faced SSTI attacks
weekly, with cloud-hosted organisations experiencing 30\% more
frequent attacks than on-premises environments. Despite this
prevalence, SSTI is rarely included in educational CTF platforms.
CTF5 addresses this gap by implementing a Jinja2/Flask environment
in which players progress from information disclosure through
basic template injection to WAF-bypassed RCE via
\texttt{\_\_globals\_\_} traversal,
modelling the escalation path that practitioners encounter in
real-world engagements.

Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) entered the 2021 OWASP Top
10 on community consensus about its severity in cloud-native
architectures, despite a relatively low measured incidence
rate of 2.72\% in the 2021 release; in the 2025 release
candidate~\cite{OWASP2025} SSRF has been consolidated into
A01 Broken Access Control, reflecting both updated incidence
data and category-boundary refinements. The 2019 Capital One
breach, analysed by
Khan et al.~\cite{khan2022}, exemplifies the catastrophic potential
of the class: an attacker exploited an SSRF flaw to query the AWS
Instance Metadata Service (IMDSv1), obtained role credentials, and
exfiltrated over 100 million records. Khan et al.\ identify a
critical design flaw in IMDSv1, namely the service's implicit trust
of any local process request, which AWS subsequently mitigated with the
session-token authentication of IMDSv2~\cite{IMDSv2}. CTF6 models
exactly this scenario, introducing a deliberately-IMDSv1-compatible
metadata mock and a second pivot into an unauthenticated Redis
service via the \texttt{dict://} scheme (suitable for
single-command Redis interaction; \texttt{gopher://} is the
canonical pivot for full RCE payloads but is out of scope
here), recreating the defence-in-depth failures that
enabled the real incident.

Insecure deserialisation is a third under-represented class. It
sits within A08 (Software and Data Integrity Failures) and is
exemplified by CVE-2017-5941~\cite{CVE20175941,NodeSerialize},
wherein the \texttt{node-serialize} library's
\texttt{unserialize()} routine evaluates serialised function
bodies, producing arbitrary code execution when a
\texttt{\_\$\$ND\_FUNC\$\$\_}-tagged immediately-invoked function
expression (IIFE) is passed in. The class receives less attention
in educational platforms than SQLi or XSS, yet remains a frequent
source of RCE in production Node.js applications. CTF7 is built
directly on this CVE to introduce students to the class.


\subsection{Personalised Challenge Environments and Academic Integrity}
Two distinct prior approaches to per-student CTF
parameterisation establish the lineage in which the present
work sits. The earlier line, exemplified by Burket et
al.'s~\cite{Burket2015} automatic problem generation in
PicoCTF, varies challenge \emph{instances} per team using
templated parameter substitution (different keys, ciphertexts,
flag tails); the resulting flags differ but the methodology is
shared. The more recent and infrastructurally heavier line is
Schreuders et al.'s SecGen framework~\cite{secgen2017}, which
parameterises virtual machines at build time to produce
per-student environments with different ports, service
configurations, and vulnerability profiles. SecGen addresses
both flag sharing and methodology sharing, but each student
receives a separate VM image that must be built, distributed,
and reset across a cohort, and its randomisation vocabulary is
restricted to the configuration-template surface, which makes
modelling application-layer vulnerabilities (SSTI payloads,
SQL filter bypasses, SSRF target topology) awkward.

The present project positions itself between the two: flags
are personalised deterministically via HMAC-SHA256 with the
username embedded in every flag string (Burket-style flag
attribution), but the per-deployment salt also rotates the
flag space across cohorts in a way the build-time PicoCTF
generator does not. Full environment randomisation of the
SecGen kind is intentionally not attempted. The scope of the
defence is explicit: the HMAC layer makes flag-copying
attributable and LLM-fabricated flags structurally invalid,
but it does not defend against \emph{methodology sharing}
(verbatim write-ups still apply); the methodology-sharing risk
is mitigated structurally by multi-stage chains in CTF5, CTF6,
and CTF9, where executing the chain still requires acting on
each stage's output against the student's own personalised
instance. The trade-off is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:flag-personalisation}.


\subsection{Containerisation and Secure Challenge Distribution}
Modern CTF deployments increasingly use Docker containers rather
than full virtual machines for scalability, resource efficiency,
and isolation. Raj et al.~\cite{raj2016} introduced a Docker-based
CTF infrastructure, showing that containerisation significantly
reduces organiser overhead and automates setup. Because containers
share the host kernel, running many instances is far more efficient
than spawning VMs, and a single host can support a large cohort.
Sasiwanit et al.~\cite{Sasiwanit} validate this approach in their
CTFd-integrated platform and demonstrate through Apache JMeter
stress testing that the architecture remains responsive under load.

Neither paper addresses the question of flag data residing
\textit{within} the container image. Both assume a centralised
hosting model in which students connect to a remote service rather
than running containers locally. This project targets a different
deployment model: students may run the Docker Compose stack on
their own machine, so the image itself must not contain the flag
material for any particular student. The containerisation strategy
described in Section~\ref{sec:containerisation} treats flag and
credential material as runtime configuration mounted from the host,
not as part of the built image.


\subsection{LLM-Based Evaluation of CTF Challenges}
Ji et al.~\cite{ji2025} report that LLMs possess substantial
technical knowledge about vulnerability classes but struggle
to apply it to specific scenarios and to adapt strategies to
environmental feedback. With appropriate scaffolding
(retrieval-augmented generation and interactive environmental
augmentation), their CTFAgent framework reports over 80\%
\emph{relative} improvement over baseline LLMs on two CTF
benchmarks and a top-23.6\% picoCTF~2024 finish. The
implication for the present work is that an LLM's difficulty
on any given challenge depends heavily on whether it sees a
raw prompt or an agentic harness with tool access; any LLM
evaluation must therefore control for and report the
scaffolding used. The finding is corroborated by Project
Naptime~\cite{Naptime2024}, which lifted CyberSecEval2
buffer-overflow scores from 5\% to 100\% with agent
scaffolding combined with tool use and Naptime@20 sampling,
and by PentestGPT~\cite{Deng2024PentestGPT}, which reports
+228.6\% over single-shot GPT-3.5.

Two complementary benchmarks anchor the LLM-CTF literature.
Shao et al.'s NYU CTF Bench~\cite{Shao2024} provides 200 CSAW
challenges with an agent framework and is the most relevant
prior benchmark to the present work. CyberMetric~\cite{Tihanyi2024}
measures LLM knowledge of cybersecurity concepts at the factual
level, independent of exploitation skill. The distinction matters
for the evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:results}: LLMs
that score highly on knowledge tests can still fail on CTF6
because the evaluation requires them to discover
\texttt{dict://}-based Redis pivoting in a specific simulated
network, not merely to recall that such pivots exist. The
EnIGMA agent~\cite{Abramovich2025}, SOTA on NYU CTF Bench at
publication, reports that ``Claude and GPT-4 Turbo did not
solve any web instance,'' which directly anchors the
web-exploitation 0\%-class results in the present trial as
consistent with frontier-LLM behaviour rather than a unique
artefact of this suite.


\subsection{Summary of Findings}
Five gaps motivate the design that follows. Empirical evidence
for measurable learning gain in graded, web-specific CTF
coursework remains limited; modern exploit classes (SSTI, SSRF,
insecure deserialisation) are rare in educational platforms
despite real-world prevalence; secure distribution of
containerised challenges without baking flag material into the
image is unaddressed in the containerised CTF literature;
SecGen-style per-student environment randomisation is too
infrastructurally heavy for web-only coursework; and LLM-CTF
performance is dominated by agentic scaffolding and so must be
reported under a controlled harness. This project addresses
each gap directly: broad OWASP coverage with under-taught
classes, a deterministic-HMAC personalisation layer lighter
than SecGen, container-native distribution with runtime-mounted
flags, and a triangulated evaluation combining automated
regression, an executed multi-model agentic LLM trial (127 paid
runs across a 5-model cold-probe and a 4-model primary panel
under a hard \pounds20 budget), and a structured human
walkthrough.
